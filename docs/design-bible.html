01 — Thesis

The game that should exist and doesn't

Across the whole mobile genre, breadth and speed have never appeared in the same product.

Football Chairman Pro 2 proved that a season can take 15–20 minutes and a career to the top can take 40–50 hours. That ratio is the genre's most important number: the season is the unit of progress, and it fits inside a commute. But the game is thin — squad members are rows in a table, not characters.

At the other end, the games with real breadth either monetise it into a mess, or they are PC-only and dead. Football Manager Mobile has squad depth but no club — no stadium, no board, no sponsorship, no ownership. And nothing on mobile generates narrative at all, despite narrative being the proven addiction driver of the entire genre.

The gap

A premium, offline, story-generating club dynasty sim with FIFA Manager's breadth of decisions at Football Chairman's cost per decision. Nobody has shipped it.

The three commitments

Simple to play

Two taps from launch to a played match. Every decision is 1–3 taps. Nothing important sits more than two taps from the hub.

Deep in consequence

Breadth lives in optional overrides on top of good defaults — never in extra navigation. The game plays itself competently; your job is to disagree with it.

Honest

No currency, no energy, no timers, nothing that buys outcomes. The engine never knows which club is yours. Losses are explained, not excused.

02 — The loop

What a player actually does

Designed backwards from the twenty-second session, because that is the session that decides retention.

The minimum path: open app → hub, with the primary button already under the thumb → tap it → resolve nought to three action cards, one tap each → match → result → tap continue → close app. Two mandatory taps between launch and a played match. Everything else is an opt-in detour, never a gate.

The hub is continue-first

Three hub models exist in this genre and two of them fail on a phone. Inbox-first (classic Football Manager) makes the player's first act reading a wall of undifferentiated text. Dashboard fails the two-second test — a widget grid has no primary action, so a newcomer doesn't know what they're supposed to do. We use continue-first with a triaged action queue.

01
The button names the event, never "Continue" "Play Matchday 12", "Open the transfer window", "Start pre-season". A bare "Continue" tells a newcomer nothing.
02
Only time-limited, outcome-changing items become cards Injuries, contracts inside six months, an accepted bid, a board mandate. Congratulations and media chatter are inbox-only — never a card, never a badge.
03
An empty queue is a feature The space collapses and the button rises further into the thumb arc. It says: nothing needs you, go and play.
04
Autosave on every hub return There is no "save" concept anywhere in the UI. The app is never in a state where closing it loses progress.

The manager's week is the content

Real managers lose their tactical prep day and their player one-to-ones entirely in a two-game week — there simply isn't time. So fixture congestion deletes decision slots rather than adding a fatigue number. It is structural pressure with zero additional UI, and it is why a cup run should feel like it costs something.

03 — The UX law

The game plays itself. Your job is to disagree with it.

This single sentence resolves the brief's central tension, and everything in the interface follows from it.

A newcomer never disagrees and finishes a season perfectly happily. A fanatic disagrees fifty times a season. Both use identical screens. Depth is not a second interface — it is a drawer.

What a newcomer sees

An assistant recommendation with a star on it. Auto-pick. Four visible ratings. A team style, not instructions. One meter for club finance. Accepting is always one tap.

SAME SCREEN

What a fanatic opens

An ADVANCED ▾ drawer that expands in place, never navigates, and remembers its state per screen forever. Attribute values, wage structures, contract clauses, scouting filters.

Non-negotiables

  • Instant Result ships on day one, on the main speed control, not buried in settings. The player who skips thirty matches to reach the derby that matters is still playing.
  • Never show a screen with no pre-selected default. Every screen arrives already answered.
  • Never punish delegation. Auto-pick and Instant Result must be genuinely competent. If delegating loses games, the whole model collapses.
  • Never gate depth behind a difficulty setting. Difficulty changes the world, not the interface.
  • Maximum three match interruptions, each pausing behind a half-height sheet. Zero in Instant.
  • One notification a day, hard cap. Never "you haven't played in 3 days". Never a countdown.

Hard accessibility numbers

48×48dp tap targets throughout (clears Apple's 44pt and WCAG AAA simultaneously). 11pt type floor with Dynamic Type to ~310%. Primary actions confined to the bottom 55% of the screen — roughly half of all phone use is one-handed. Never a confirm button in the top-right corner.

04 — Simulation

Simple choices, believable outcomes

Seven visible attributes on the surface. A calibrated xG event simulator underneath.

The player

Seven visible attributes — Pace, Technical, Vision, Finishing, Defending, Physical, Composure — with goalkeepers using the same seven slots relabelled, so there is one schema and one UI component in the entire game. Overall ability is derived from role-weighted sums, never stored, which means a player's rating automatically depends on where you play him — position familiarity for free, with no separate system.

Eight hidden bytes carry the personality: potential, consistency, injury proneness, professionalism, ambition, adaptability, big-game temperament. That is the whole narrative engine. It is enough to generate the wonderkid who stalls, the pro who plays until 37, and the mercenary who agitates every January.

Decline is emergent, not special-cased

Ageing is weighted by how much of a player's ability sits in Pace and Physical. That is the entire reason a 33-year-old playmaker stays useful and a 33-year-old winger doesn't. Nobody wrote a rule for it.

The match

An xG-driven possession-event simulator, deliberately shallow — six states, not forty — calibrated so its aggregate output reproduces a Dixon-Coles model. Pure Poisson gives you a scoreline but no story. A full zonal chain buries most of its fidelity behind a pitch view we are not rendering. The event sim is the only option that yields a watchable feed and provably realistic scorelines.

Drama comes from two mechanisms and no dice-rolls: momentum is a mean-reverting process, so the feed produces periods of pressure rather than a uniform drip of chances; and urgency keys off the deficit and the clock, which produces late equalisers, late sucker-punch counters and the throwing-bodies-forward feel — all from the score, all symmetric, all explainable. There is no upset code. Underdogs already win about a quarter of the time from the maths alone.

Tactics: one screen, hard limit

A formation and three sliders — Tempo, Line, Mentality. Nothing else. The rock-paper-scissors comes from four interaction terms, each readable in one sentence:

Your approachBeatsWhy, in one sentence
High pressPatient build-upYou win it back before they get going.
DirectHigh pressYou go over the top of the press.
PatientDeep blockA block with no outlet eventually cracks.
Deep blockDirectYou are already defending your own box.

Reading the opposition is worth roughly 4–6 ability points across the XI — meaningful, never decisive. Explicitly rejected: player instructions, individual roles, set-piece routines, opposition instruction screens. Each needs a second screen and buys nothing the four counters don't.

Fairness is a build artefact, not a promise

The match function never receives a flag telling it which club is the player's. This is enforced by a unit test that simulates the same fixture with the flag on both sides and compares the distributions. Difficulty presets change starting budget, board patience, and how well AI clubs search the market. They never touch the maths.

The market must not break at season eight

This is where every game in the genre dies. The rule: an AI club's maximum bid derives only from the valuation formula — never from another club's bid, and never from what someone paid last week. That kills the price ratchet at its root. Backed by a global index that inflates revenues and prices together, a hard wage-to-revenue cap, permanent money sinks, and a season-end drift monitor that self-corrects at ±12%.

Corrections made during the build

The research spec's shot-rate constant produced 0.70 goals per team per match — roughly half of real football. The stated possession count and the stated rate were incompatible. Fixed by measurement, not by argument.

Club strength was also spread far too wide: the first pass had the best side beating the worst 91.8% of the time. A real top division spans about fifteen ability points, not twenty-six. Compressing the spread brought it to 77.2%.

And the calibration harness itself was flawed — it generated its test squads before reseeding, so two runs of the same code returned different numbers. Any figure measured before that fix was noise.

The residual draw inflation is a known, accepted deviation: momentum autocorrelation nudges evenly-matched fixtures toward level. Real football is itself over-dispersed toward draws relative to Poisson, so some of this is correct — but three points is more than Dixon-Coles alone would explain, and it should be revisited when the full league pyramid exists.

06 — The unlicensed world

How close to reality we can legally get

Not legal advice. Commission a Class 9/41 clearance search before any identity spend.

The one non-negotiable

Real player names are out. Not risky — out. "Name only, no likeness" is not a defence: name plus club plus nationality plus age plus position plus attributes is the likeness. Two US cases rejected the free-speech defence specifically because the games recreated athletes in the setting where they are famous — which is exactly what a management sim is.

Why this is stricter than a normal legal caveat. A trade mark complaint against a mobile game is not a lawsuit, it is a takedown. Copyright has a counter-notice route; trade mark has a support queue. Google's remedy for suspension is to resubmit under a new package name — new listing, zero reviews, zero ranking, broken installs for every existing player. One brand-protection analyst can trigger it.

The safe zone is generous

Yes

Real cities and districts. Real stadium capacities and geography. League formats copied exactly — promotion, relegation, group stages. Real financial scales. Real calendar rhythm. Real nationalities and name distributions. Fixture lists we generate ourselves.

No

Club names, crests, kit designs. Competition names and logos. Real player names or likenesses. Any commercial data feed, even for data that is itself unprotected — that is a contract problem, not a copyright one.

Two findings that change the build

Fixture lists are safe. European courts held they attract neither database right nor database copyright, and the US has no database right at all. The residual risk is purely contractual, so we generate our own and never touch a licensed feed.

The exposure is our relationship to renaming mods, not the mods themselves. Manchester United's claim against Sports Interactive reached into SI's support for mods that restored the crest — and it argued that omitting the crest was itself a harm. The rule that follows: ship a first-class editor and say nothing about real names anywhere. Not in marketing, not in support replies, not in Discord. Never host renaming packs on our own infrastructure.

Making fake clubs feel real

Fake clubs fail because they are arbitrary, not because they are fake. The fix is a grammar rather than a random generator: a six-layer heraldic crest system — shields, field divisions, ordinaries, charges — producing over half a million structurally distinct forms, with a rejection sampler guaranteeing no two clubs in a league are confusable. The emotional payload is per-nation weighting: an English club draws a heater shield with a lion or a rose and a scroll; a German club gets a roundel, a monogram, and no furniture. Kit colours derive from the crest's own tinctures, so crest, kit and league-table row all cohere.

Player faces are layered vector avatars, not AI portraits — partly for art consistency, but mainly because the EU AI Act's machine-marking obligations and New York's synthetic-performer disclosure law both bite in 2026, and the fiction carve-out is assessed per image rather than per category.

07 — Look

Floodlight

Premium dark editorial, with broadcast punctuation.

The baseline is quiet and editorial so that dense data can breathe. Then, four or five times a session, the screen becomes a broadcast graphic — amber slab, diagonal cut, huge condensed scoreline. Full broadcast realism was rejected because it fights a product made of forty-row lists and it flattens the emotional peaks by making everything loud.

Ground

#0A0C0F canvas over four surface layers. Dark is the default and the brand; light ships later as a token remap, never a redesign.

Accent

#FFC53D amber — chosen because green, red and grey are already spoken for by win, loss and draw, the most-read colours in the product.

Type

Archivo for display and scorelines, Inter for body and data. Both SIL OFL 1.1, verified upstream. Tabular figures mandatory on every number.

Colour is never the only channel. Every win/draw/loss chip carries its letter, every form arrow carries a glyph, every money delta carries a sign. Roughly 8% of male players have a colour-vision deficiency and win/loss red-green is precisely the axis that fails them.

Tabular figures are not a detail. With proportional digits a league table's points column shimmers as you scroll, and a number ticking from 9 to 10 shifts everything after it.

The mark

An original two-handled cup on a stepped plinth, drawn as flat geometry at monumental scale on an amber ground. The art direction's ban on gradients and skeuomorphism decided the execution: "engraved" is expressed structurally — the plinth is the nameplate — rather than as rendered texture. That is also what makes it survive downscaling. 48px is the verified floor for it reading as a trophy; at 29px it degrades to a recognisable dark mass on amber, which is documented rather than hidden.

08 — Money

The monetisation is the marketing

In this genre, restraint is a competitive weapon, and the review pages prove it.

Every free rival draws the same complaints, close to verbatim: a credit card making someone stronger than a player who has been there for years; everything tied to coin bundles; energy timers; offerwalls that don't pay out. Meanwhile the two best-reviewed titles in the genre are ad-free and timer-free, and reviewers cite the absence of monetisation as a feature.

09 — Contradictions

Where the research disagreed with itself

Eight specialists worked in parallel. These are the collisions, and how each was settled.

CollisionThe conflictResolution
Scripted tutorial UX wanted the first match lightly scripted for competitiveness. The engine's core principle is that it must never cheat the player. The tutorial match sits entirely outside the career, before the save's seed is locked. Scripting is permitted exactly once, and never again.
Instant Result vs fairness Skipping a match must not change its outcome, or Instant Result becomes a save-scumming tool. The match is simulated in full before playback begins. Watching is a rendering of a result that already exists. Skipping is provably free.
"It's rigged" Two agents, working from unrelated evidence — competitor reviews and simulation theory — independently landed on the same feature. The post-match driver dump. Convergence from separate evidence made this the first thing built rather than a nice-to-have.
Editor vs takedown risk Community renaming is a major draw. Both stores ban apps that "induce" infringement. Ship the editor as a first-class feature; market total silence. The feature is legal; talking about it is what creates exposure.
Breadth vs "a baby could play it" The two reference games pull in opposite directions — Football Chairman is simple because it is shallow. Depth moves into drawers, not navigation. Same screens for both audiences. This is the single idea the product rests on.

One cut I want a second opinion on

The manager-role research removed attribute-level scouting entirely — four visible ratings plus one hidden trait that reveals itself over time. I think this is right, and better drama: discovering in November that your record signing is a bottler is a story, whereas reading it off a stat screen in July is a spreadsheet. But it is the largest single departure from FIFA Manager in the whole document, and it is the one decision here most likely to be wrong.

10 — What's built

The vertical slice

A working, playable prototype — not a mockup.

Working today

Twenty procedurally generated clubs with heraldic crests. Full squads with derived ability and hidden potential. The calibrated match engine. A 38-match season with a live league table. Continue-first hub with the action queue. Squad and lineup with auto-pick. Formation and the three tactical sliders with live assistant advice. The match takeover with four speed modes including Instant. The post-match driver dump. Injuries, fatigue, form, and recovery. Board objectives you can accept or argue down. Save and resume.

Specified but not yet wired

Transfer bidding, contracts and agents (valuation is live and visible; the negotiation flow is not). Season rollover and multi-season careers. Youth intake and regens. The Club Chronicle. AI managers moving between clubs. Cup competitions. The heraldic rejection sampler that guarantees per-league distinctness. Penalties in the match engine. The CI trade mark sweep.

Known issue in the slice

The inbox accumulates without curation — after a dozen matchdays it holds fifty-plus items, which directly violates the notification discipline set out in section 03. The queue logic needs to apply to the inbox too, not just the hub cards.

11 — Build order

What to do next, in order

The sequence matters: each step de-risks the one after it.

01
Multi-season careers Season rollover, ageing, youth intake, contract expiry. Until a save survives ten seasons, nothing else can be trusted — and this is where the genre's competitors break.
02
The transfer market end to end Bidding, wages, agents, clauses, loans — plus the drift monitor. Run fifteen simulated seasons and check prices are flat in real terms.
03
The Club Chronicle The auto-written history that turns a 40-hour save into something the player owns. Nobody on mobile does this, and it is the cheapest genuine differentiator in the document.
04
The CI trade mark sweep Fails the build on a blocklist hit. The one legal rule that needs engineering, and the one that makes the other twenty-one self-enforcing rather than aspirational.
05
Native shell Only once the loop is proven addictive on the web build. Porting an unproven loop is the most expensive mistake available here.
